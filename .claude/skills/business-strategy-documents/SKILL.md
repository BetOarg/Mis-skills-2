# Business Strategy and Documents

## Goal
Produce commercially useful, decision-ready work grounded in evidence. Make the document usable by its intended audience and clear about what is confirmed, proposed, or still to be negotiated.

## Rules
- Identify the decision to support, audience, geography, time horizon, and intended format.
- Use user-provided facts faithfully. Verify external or changing facts when research is available.
- Clearly label **CONFIRMED**, **ASSUMPTION/PROPOSAL**, and **PENDING CONFIRMATION**.
- Never invent prices, commissions, fees, contract parties, delivery dates, legal obligations, financing terms, market statistics, testimonials, or performance outcomes.
- Do not turn a proposed negotiation point into an agreed term. Preserve distinctions between buyer costs, seller/developer costs, commission, honoraria, advertising spend, and taxes.
- For legal or contract-related documents, identify issues and draft commercial language for review; do not represent it as jurisdiction-specific legal advice or a substitute for local counsel.
- Tailor language and currency conventions to the market and audience (for example, Rioplatense Spanish where requested).
- State sources, date, and limitations for external data. If evidence is unavailable, mark the claim as needing validation.
- Keep recommendations actionable: rationale, options, trade-offs, dependencies, owner, and next step.
- Do not send, sign, publish, or share externally without explicit approval.
- Avoid unnecessary personal data and do not expose confidential business information.

## Workflow
1. **Define the decision:** what needs to be decided, by whom, and by when.
2. **Inventory facts:** capture user-provided and verified facts; mark unknowns and conflicting terms.
3. **Research:** if appropriate, verify the market, competitors, pricing, regulations, audience, and comparable offers. Cite sources for sourced claims.
4. **Frame options:** compare viable approaches, benefits, costs, risks, dependencies, and reversibility.
5. **Recommend:** select a preferred option and explain why, while noting conditions that could change the recommendation.
6. **Draft deliverable:** use a structure suited to the artifact—executive summary, objectives, market/offer, plan, economics, responsibilities, risks, and next steps as relevant.
7. **Commercial and legal check:** verify names, amounts, currencies, payment terms, deadlines, commissions, responsibilities, cancellation, data handling, and jurisdiction; leave unresolved fields clearly marked.
8. **Consistency check:** ensure no contradictions between executive summary, tables, assumptions, and conclusion.
9. **Usability check:** make it easy to copy, edit, present, or use from a phone. Use concise headings, readable tables, and complete copy-ready language.
10. **Final report:** list what is ready, what requires confirmation, key risks, and the next action. Do not claim approval or execution that has not occurred.

## Recommended document sections
Use only relevant sections, but consider:
- Executive summary and decision requested.
- Confirmed context and objectives.
- Target customer and value proposition.
- Options and competitive position.
- Recommended strategy and action plan.
- Budget/economics with assumptions visible.
- Responsibilities and commercial terms.
- Risks, dependencies, and mitigations.
- Pending confirmations.
- Immediate next steps and decision deadline.

## Examples

### Good example 1
User: “Prepare a proposal for representing a property development.”
Action: separate confirmed developer-provided material and target markets from unconfirmed commission, fees, prices, payment methods, and delivery commitments. Draft the proposal with clear placeholders and a negotiation checklist; do not imply the contract is signed.

### Good example 2
User: “Compare three investment options.”
Action: establish comparable cost, timeframe, risk, liquidity, and assumptions; cite sourced figures, expose missing inputs, and provide a recommendation conditional on the user's priorities.

### Bad example
User: “Write the commercial agreement.”
Bad action: invent a commission percentage, legal entity name, delivery guarantee, and payment terms to make the document look complete.
Why bad: it converts unknowns into apparent commitments and may create legal or financial exposure.
